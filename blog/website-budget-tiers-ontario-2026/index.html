What a $1,000, $2,000 and $5,000 website gets you in Ontario

Published October 2, 2026 • By Rujal Tuladhar

You have a number in your head for a website. Maybe it is $1,000, maybe $5,000, and you cannot tell whether a builder will find it generous or laughable. Ask three shops and you can get quotes thousands of dollars apart for what sounds like the same five pages.

This post draws on seven 2026 pricing guides from Toronto and Ontario web shops, plus the official price pages of the platforms small sites run on. Here is what they say, where they contradict each other, and what about $1,000, $2,000 or $5,000 usually covers. Treat every price as before HST unless a quote says otherwise. Webbies, Shift8 Web and parts of the Cressoft guide label their figures as Canadian dollars; the other Ontario shops do not state a currency, so I have read them as Canadian too.

What a $1,000, $2,000 and $5,000 website gets you in Ontario at a glance — the short version.

What Ontario web shops publish for 2026

Start with the cheapest professional tier. Forge Web (April 1, 2026) puts a template site of three to five pages at $500 to $1,500. GrowthBoss (updated September 11, 2026) describes much the same scope at $1,500 to $4,000, Webbies (updated July 31, 2026) at $1,000 to $5,000, and Lume Creative (June 8, 2026) at $1,500 to $5,500 and up.

Toronto adds another layer. Cressoft (updated August 2026) compares one five-page business site across builders: $800 to $3,000 from a freelancer, $1,499 to $3,000 from a boutique studio and $10,000 to $25,000 from a large Toronto agency. Shift8 Web (April 23, 2026) prices a Toronto brochure site of one to five pages at $3,000 to $7,500 CAD. George Barakat (updated August 5, 2026) estimates Toronto pricing at 20 to 40 percent above the Ontario average.

These sources disagree, and none is necessarily wrong. Each describes its own costs and its own idea of a website, and several are quoting their own packages.

What it means for you: Treat a published range as one shop's price list, not a market rate. Write down your scope first (pages, who writes the text, what the site has to do), then ask every builder to quote that same scope.

About $1,000: a small template site

At this price you are usually buying a template set up properly, not a design drawn for your business. Forge Web's lowest tier is a fair summary: three to five pages, a contact form, a mobile-responsive template and basic hosting setup, without custom design, SEO work or ongoing support. Expect to supply the words and photos; Forge Web lists content writing as an extra at $50 to $150 per page.

The real alternative is building it yourself. Squarespace's pricing page shows its Basic plan at CA$21 a month paid annually or CA$30 month to month, which works out to roughly $250 to $360 a year before tax, plus your own evenings. If you plan to draft the page text with an AI tool, our free AI course for beginners is a sensible first stop.

For comparison, our entry package at Nova Toronto is Bronze: three pages for $900 plus applicable tax. Who should skip this tier: anyone who has not yet settled what they sell and to whom. A template cannot fix an unclear offer.

What it means for you: About $1,000 buys three to five template pages and a contact form. Budget your own time for text and photos, and confirm in writing whether hosting setup is included.

About $2,000: five pages you can measure

Around $2,000 the site starts being built to be found and measured. Forge Web says its $1,000 to $3,000 tier is where most Ontario small businesses should start. Cressoft's $1,499 to $3,500 tier is typically five to eight pages and includes basic on-page SEO and Google Analytics setup.

Two things separate this tier from the last. The first is analytics. Google Analytics is free of charge, so you are paying for setup: making sure a form submission or a tap on your phone number is counted. Without that, you cannot tell whether the site produced an enquiry.

The second is on-page SEO. Google's SEO Starter Guide covers the basics: a unique, accurate title for each page, a short meta description (the guide describes it as typically a one- or two-sentence summary), and URLs with words a reader would find useful. The same guide says there are "no secrets" that automatically put a site first, and that some changes can take several months to show. On-page SEO makes a site eligible to rank. It is not a ranking campaign.

Our Silver package sits here: five pages with analytics and SEO for $1,800 plus applicable tax.

What it means for you: At about $2,000, ask the builder to show where analytics will record a lead and to list the title and description planned for each page. If neither is in the quote, you are paying second-tier money for a first-tier site.

$5,000 and up: when custom work earns its price

Custom should mean layout and features designed around your business, not adapted from a theme. The word is used loosely: Forge Web and Cressoft both describe tiers under $3,500 as custom-designed, so ask each builder what it covers. George Barakat puts a custom small-business site in Ontario at $5,000 to $15,000; GrowthBoss gives $5,000 to $20,000 with strategy and copy included.

That spend is justified when you can name what a template cannot do:

  • The site has a job. Forge Web's $2,500 to $5,000-plus tier adds online booking, CRM integration and automated review requests.
  • You need the copy written, or many services and locations each need a page.
  • You employ 50 or more people. Ontario's accessibility guidance says businesses and non-profits of that size, and designated public sector organizations, must make their public websites and web content posted after January 1, 2012 meet WCAG 2.0 Level AA, with two exceptions (live captions and pre-recorded audio descriptions). That work belongs in the quote. This is a summary, not legal advice; confirm how the rules apply to you with a lawyer or an accessibility specialist.

It is not justified for five pages and a form, or while the offer is still changing. Selling products does not automatically mean custom either. Shopify's Canadian pricing shows Basic at $49 CAD a month, or $37 CAD a month billed yearly, and WooCommerce is free, open-source software for WordPress that you host yourself, so hosting is a separate bill. Agency ranges for stores run higher: Cressoft lists $5,000 to $15,000, and GrowthBoss $10,000 to $25,000 and up, scaling with catalogue size. A small catalogue can cost less: our ecommerce package is $2,000 plus applicable tax on Shopify or WooCommerce, and custom work is $5,000 plus applicable tax.

What it means for you: Pay for custom when you can name the function or the content a template cannot deliver. If you cannot name it yet, the $2,000 site is the right purchase this year.

What sits outside the build quote

HST. The Canada Revenue Agency's rate table shows 13% for Ontario, and Webbies notes its ranges are usually before tax. A builder who charges HST turns $1,000 into $1,130, $2,000 into $2,260 and $5,000 into $5,650. Ask your accountant how the tax affects your own books.

Domain and platform fees. GrowthBoss and George Barakat both put a domain at $15 to $30 a year. The Squarespace and Shopify subscriptions above keep running as long as the site does.

Hosting and upkeep. The published numbers are far apart: $200 to $1,500 a year (George Barakat), $100 to $150 a month for maintenance (Forge Web), $70 to $200 a month for a professionally managed site (GrowthBoss), and $1,400 to $7,000 CAD a year (Shift8 Web).

What it means for you: Ask every builder for the first-year total: build, HST, domain, hosting, platform subscription and maintenance. Compare that figure, not the build fee.

Seven questions that make quotes comparable

Quotes differ mostly because they describe different things. Send every builder the same list:

  1. How many pages, and who writes the text and supplies the photos?
  2. Template or custom design, and on which platform?
  3. Is analytics installed, with form submissions and phone taps tracked?
  4. What on-page SEO is done on each page?
  5. Will the domain, hosting account and site files be in my name?
  6. What do I pay each month or year after launch, and what does it cover?
  7. How many revision rounds, what launch date, and is HST in the price?

Written answers to all seven give you quotes you can compare.

What it means for you: Paste these seven questions into your quote request. Two or three written answers side by side will tell you more than any published range.

The bottom line

The short version: about $1,000 buys a small template site that proves you exist; about $2,000 buys five pages that can be found and measured; $5,000 and up buys design, copy and features built around your business. Forge Web and Cressoft both point most small businesses to the middle range, and for a service business starting out I agree. Add 13% HST and a year of running costs before comparing.

Common questions

How much does a five-page website cost in Ontario in 2026?

It depends on who builds it. Cressoft's Toronto comparison lists a five-page business site at $800 to $3,000 from a freelancer, $1,499 to $3,000 from a boutique studio and $10,000 to $25,000 from a large agency. Nova Toronto's five-page Silver package with analytics and SEO is $1,800 plus applicable tax.

What is the difference between a $1,000 and a $2,000 website?

Mostly measurement and search basics. Near $1,000 you usually get three to five template pages and a contact form, using text and photos you supply. Near $2,000 you should also get analytics set up, plus a unique title and description on every page.

What does a website cost each year after it is built?

Sources disagree. A domain is about $15 to $30 a year. For upkeep, George Barakat says $200 to $1,500 a year, GrowthBoss says $70 to $200 a month for a professionally managed site, and Shift8 Web says $1,400 to $7,000 CAD a year. Ask for a first-year total.

Does my business website have to meet accessibility rules in Ontario?

Ontario's guidance says businesses and non-profits with 50 or more employees, and designated public sector organizations, must make public websites and web content posted after January 1, 2012 meet WCAG 2.0 Level AA, with two exceptions. If you are at or near that size, ask for the accessibility work to be written into the quote and confirm your obligations with a professional.

What AI Actually Is (and What It Is Not)

By the end of this lesson: Explain in one sentence what an AI chat tool does, name three things it is good at and three things it gets wrong, and hold a first real conversation with one.

Welcome. If you have barely touched AI, you are in the right place. Nothing in this lesson needs a credit card or a technical background. The reading takes about 12 minutes and the exercise about 10.

By the end you will know what an AI chat tool really is, where it helps and where it slips. You will also have had your first proper conversation with one, and you will test it with a question it may well get wrong, so that a confident mistake never catches you off guard later.

The plain-English picture

An AI chat tool is a very well-read text predictor. It has been trained on a huge amount of writing. When you type a message, it builds a reply a few words at a time, choosing the words that are most likely to fit.

Here is your one-sentence version: an AI chat tool reads what you type and writes the most likely helpful reply, based on patterns in the text it learned from.

Two things follow from that.

  • It does not know your business. It has never seen your price list, your booking calendar or your customers.
  • It does not always look things up. Most tools now have a web search feature. When search is not used, the reply comes only from training, and training stops at a certain date.

A helpful comparison is a new hire on their first day. They are fast, well read and happy to draft anything you ask for. They have also never met your customers, do not know how you do things, and will sometimes say something wrong in a completely confident voice. You would not let that person send a quote without reading it first. Treat the AI tool the same way.

Where it helps and where it slips

AI chat tools are good with words. Six jobs they do well:

  • First drafts of emails, notices and posts
  • Rewording a message in a friendlier or firmer tone
  • Summarizing long text into a few lines
  • Explaining something in simpler words
  • Listing ideas, such as names for a seasonal special
  • Tidying messy notes into a clean list or table

They are weak in places that matter to a business:

  • Arithmetic on long lists. Adding up thirty invoice lines in a plain chat can go wrong. Use a calculator or a spreadsheet.
  • Recent events. Without web search, it only has its training, which stops at a certain date.
  • Your local details. Your hours, your prices, the parking on your street.
  • Legal, medical and tax decisions. It can explain a term. It cannot take responsibility for a decision, and it does not replace a professional.
  • Admitting doubt. It can sound just as sure when it is wrong as when it is right.

That last weakness has a name. A hallucination is an answer the tool has made up but states with confidence: a wrong date, an invented quote, a source that does not exist.

This is not a rumour. The companies say it themselves. OpenAI's help page Does ChatGPT tell the truth? explains that it can sound sure when it is wrong. Anthropic has a similar page for Claude. Microsoft's Copilot terms tell you to check its answers before you act. And Google prints a warning on the Gemini chat page: "Gemini is AI and can make mistakes."

Which free tool to open

Four well-known chat tools offer a free plan. We checked each company's own pages on October 2, 2026. Free plans change often, so treat this as a snapshot.

  • ChatGPT (from OpenAI) at chatgpt.com. You can try a basic chat without logging in. Logging in with a free account adds saved chats, file uploads and picture-making. OpenAI says everyday text chats are unlimited on the free plan. Uploads, pictures and voice have their own smaller limits. Minimum age is 13, with a parent's permission if you are under 18.
  • Claude (from Anthropic) at claude.ai. You need a free account and a mobile number that can receive a text code. The free plan includes chat, web search and file creation. Its usage limit resets every five hours, and the number of messages you get varies with demand. Picture-making is not on its list of features. You must be 18 or older.
  • Google Gemini at gemini.google.com. Some features work without signing in. For the rest you use a personal Google account, such as a Gmail address. Minimum age is 13, or the age set for your country. The free plan includes picture-making. Its limit refreshes every five hours until you reach a weekly limit.
  • Microsoft Copilot at copilot.microsoft.com. You can sign in with a Microsoft, Apple or Google account. The free version includes chat and answers based on web search. Signing in adds chat history and picture-making. Minimum age is 13, higher in some countries.

All four also have apps that are free to download from the Canadian App Store and Google Play.

What is a limit? Free plans ration heavier use. When you reach a limit, the tool tells you and you wait for it to reset. You are not charged. Each tool also sells paid plans. You do not need one for this course.

How to choose: if you already use Gmail, Gemini is the least effort. If you have a Microsoft account, try Copilot, but read its terms first: Microsoft's terms for the individual version say it is for your own personal use, which Lesson 5 comes back to. Otherwise pick ChatGPT or Claude. Then stay with that one tool for the whole course, so you learn its habits. The one exception is Lesson 5, which is about pictures: Claude does not make them, so Claude users will borrow a second tool for that lesson.

The one rule: AI drafts, you decide

One rule carries through this whole course: AI drafts, you decide.

The tool can write the email. You are the one who signs it. It can suggest wording for a quote. You are the one who sends it and answers to the customer if a number is wrong. "The AI told me" will not settle a complaint.

A second habit starts today: keep private details out of the chat. Do not type or paste customer names, phone numbers, addresses, health details, card numbers or passwords. ChatGPT's own home page tells you not to share sensitive information and says chats may be reviewed. Lesson 6 covers what is safe to share.

For a small business in the Greater Toronto Area, the first step is small. You do not need to buy software or hire a consultant to begin. You need two things:

  • one free tool, and
  • the habit of treating everything it gives you as a draft.

A plumber in Scarborough, a dental office in Mississauga and a café in Leslieville can all start the same way: ask for a draft, read it with a critical eye, fix what is wrong, and only then use it.

Try it: Your first three-message conversation (about 10 minutes)

  1. Open the free AI chat tool you picked, in a web browser or its phone app. If creating an account takes more than a couple of minutes, ChatGPT lets you do this exercise without logging in. Do not type customer names, phone numbers or any private details. Lesson 6 covers what is safe to share.
  2. Send Message 1 (the first prompt below), filling in your type of business and your city. Read the reply and pick one task you would actually hand over.
  3. Send Message 2 (the second prompt below). It asks about something you already know: your own business hours and nearest main intersection. Use only details that are already public, such as what is on your sign or website. If you work from home, ask about a local shop you know well instead of giving your home address.
  4. Compare its answer with what you know to be true. Notice whether it was right, wrong, vague, or honest about not knowing.
  5. Send Message 3 (the third prompt below) to ask how confident it is and how you would check the answer.
  6. Write down three short notes: one thing it got right, one thing it got wrong or vague, and how it reacted when you challenged it. If it got everything right, note how you confirmed that. Sounding sure and being right are not the same thing, and that is the most important thing you will learn today.

Prompts you can copy

Message 1: What can you help me with?

I run a [your type of business] in [your city]. Explain to me, in plain language with no jargon, three everyday tasks you could help me with and three you should not be trusted with.

Message 2: A question you already know the answer to

What are the opening hours of [your business name] at [your street and city], and what is the nearest main intersection?

Message 3: The confidence check

How confident are you in that last answer, where did the information come from, and how would I check it?

Bonus: Explain yourself simply

In two sentences a new employee at a [your type of business] would understand, explain what you are and one reason I should double-check what you tell me.

Before you move on

Remember: An AI chat tool is a fast, well-read assistant that predicts helpful words rather than knowing facts, so it drafts and you decide.

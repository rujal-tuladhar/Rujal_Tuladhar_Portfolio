Research and Fact-Checking: Trust, but Verify

By the end of this lesson: Use AI with web search to research a business question, ask for and open the sources, and apply a simple three-step check before relying on any answer.

In the last lesson you used AI to write. Today you use it to find things out. This can save you a lot of reading time. It is also where a wrong answer can cost you real money.

You do not need to become a researcher. You need two habits: ask the tool to show where its answer came from, and spend a few minutes checking before you act on it. By the end of this lesson you will have tested one real answer yourself.

Two modes: answering from memory or searching the web

An AI chat tool can answer in two ways. It can answer from memory, using what it learned when it was built, which may be many months old. Or it can search the web, read a few pages, and answer from those.

The quickest way to tell which one you got is to look for links. An answer built from a search usually shows citations, which are small links to the pages it used. No links usually means it answered from memory.

As of October 2, 2026, the free plans of three common tools all include web search:

  • ChatGPT: it may search on its own. To start a search yourself, select View all tools and then Search, or type / and choose Search. When a Sources button appears, it lists the pages used.
  • Claude: press the + button and click Web search so a checkmark shows. In the newer version there is no switch, and Claude searches when it judges that it will help. Answers that use search include source links.
  • Gemini: when it has used web pages, a Sources button appears with the answer. Google notes that not every answer has one.

None of the three help pages gives a fixed number of free searches per day. Each free plan has usage limits, and the tool tells you when you reach them. Copilot, the fourth tool from Lesson 1, also answers from web search on its free version, so look for the links shown with its answer. If you are not sure whether your tool searched, type: "Search the web for this and show your sources."

Why a confident answer can still be wrong

In Lesson 1 you saw that AI builds answers by predicting likely words. For research, that has one consequence to remember: plausible and true are not the same thing. The tool writes what sounds right, in the same confident tone, whether it is right or not.

The usual failures are:

  • Made-up statistics
  • Wrong dates
  • Out-of-date prices and rates
  • Invented quotes, sometimes with a real person's name attached

The companies that make these tools say so themselves. OpenAI's help page on search warns that results and citations "can be incomplete, outdated, or incorrect." Anthropic and Google publish similar warnings for Claude and Gemini.

Here is a local example. Ontario's general minimum wage rose to $17.95 an hour on October 1, 2026. The day before, it was $17.60. A tool answering from memory could easily give you the old number, and it would sound just as sure of itself.

Ask for proof, then do the three-step check

You can make checking much easier by asking for proof at the start. Add these lines to any research question:

  • "Give me the source link for every claim."
  • "Quote the exact sentence you relied on."
  • "What is the date of this information?"
  • "Tell me what you are not sure about."

A link is not proof by itself. The tool can misread a page, or link to a page that does not say what it claims. Even the "exact quote" can be wrong. So before you rely on an answer, do the three-step check:

  1. Open the link. Find the sentence on the page. Does it really say that? Your browser's "Find on page" option makes this quick.
  2. Look at who published it and when. A government page updated last week is worth more than a blog post with no date.
  3. Find a second source. For anything you will act on or publish, find one independent source that agrees, ideally the original authority.

If a link is broken, or the page does not contain the claim, treat the claim as unconfirmed. Tell the tool and ask it to search again. Check the new answer the same way, because a tool will sometimes simply agree with you.

Where AI is only the starting point

Some facts are too costly to get wrong. For these, use AI to understand the topic and prepare your questions. The answer itself comes from the official page or a professional:

  • Tax rates and filing rules
  • Licences and permits
  • Employment standards, such as pay, hours, vacation and termination
  • Health claims about a product or service
  • Anything legal, such as contracts, leases and disputes

For example, the Canada Revenue Agency lists the HST rate for Ontario as 13%. An AI tool will probably tell you the same. But the rate on a particular sale can depend on what you sell and on the place of supply, which is often where your customer is. That part belongs to the CRA's own pages or your accountant.

Good research jobs for AI in a small business:

  • Comparing two software options side by side, then confirming prices on each company's own pricing page
  • Explaining a new rule in plain language
  • Preparing a list of questions before you call your accountant, lawyer or city hall
  • Summarizing a long document you own

Summarizing long documents, and what this means for your business

Free plans also let you attach a file, such as a PDF of a supplier contract or an insurance policy. As of October 2, 2026, OpenAI's help page says ChatGPT's free plan allows 3 file uploads a day. Claude's help page lists up to 20 files per chat, and Gemini's lists up to 10 files in one prompt. Free plans also have general usage limits, and all of these numbers change, so check your tool's help page.

Ask for two things: a plain-language summary, and "What would a careful reader worry about here?" The second question is the useful one. It brings up renewal dates, penalties and notice periods that are easy to miss. Then ask which section each point comes from and read those parts yourself. A summary can leave out or misstate a clause. For a contract you are about to sign, the summary helps you prepare questions. It does not replace legal advice.

Privacy note: do not paste or upload anything that contains personal information about customers, staff or anyone else. That includes names, contact details, health details and payment information. If a document has these, leave it out or remove those parts first. A contract also names the other company and holds confidential business terms, which Lesson 6 treats as material to handle with care, so check your privacy settings there before you upload one. Lesson 6 covers privacy in more detail.

You may also see a feature called Deep Research in ChatGPT and Gemini. It reads many pages and writes a longer report with sources. Google says a report usually takes about 5 to 10 minutes. How much you can use it depends on your plan, and Google says it may be unavailable at busy times without a paid plan. Claude lists its similar Research feature under paid plans only. The same three-step check applies to those reports.

For a GTA small business, the lesson is simple. AI can cut down your reading time, but only the checking step makes the result safe to rely on. One wrong number in a quote, a payroll run or a web page can cost more than the time you saved.

Try it: Fact-check one real answer

  1. Choose one question that involves a rule or a number. To finish in about 10 minutes, use one of these two: "What is the current general minimum wage in Ontario?" or "What rate of HST applies in Ontario?" (A question about your own city's rules, such as sidewalk signs, also works but may take longer.)
  2. Open your free AI chat tool and make sure web search is on (see the first section). Paste the first prompt below with your question filled in. Do not include any customer or staff details.
  3. Open up to three of the links in the answer. On each page, find the sentence that supports the claim. Write down yes or no: does the page really say what the AI said?
  4. For each link you opened, note who published the page and the date it was written or last updated.
  5. Now go to the official source yourself, without using the AI's link: ontario.ca for provincial rules, canada.ca for federal tax, or your city's own website. Find the same fact there. Answer key for the two examples, checked on October 2, 2026: Ontario's general minimum wage is $17.95 an hour (in effect October 1, 2026 to September 30, 2027), and the HST rate in Ontario is 13%.
  6. Record three results in a note: Did the AI get it right? Was every source real and did it say what the AI claimed? Was the information current? Keep this note as your personal reminder of how much to trust your tool.

Prompts you can copy

Research question with sources

I run a [type of business] in [city], Ontario. Search the web and answer this question: [your question]. Give me a source link for every claim. Quote the exact sentence you relied on from each source. Tell me the date of the information and who published it. Use official government sources where they exist. At the end, list anything you are not sure about.

Ask the tool to check its own answer

Go back over your last answer. For each claim, show me the link and the exact sentence on that page that supports it. If you cannot find a supporting sentence, say so plainly and mark that claim as unconfirmed. Also tell me whether any of this information could have changed since [month and year].

Summarize a document you own

I have attached [type of document, for example a supplier contract]. It contains no personal information about customers or staff. Summarize it in plain language in no more than [number] bullet points. Then answer: what would a careful reader worry about here? Look for renewal dates, fees, penalties, notice periods and anything unusual. For each point, tell me the section or page number so I can read it myself.

Prepare for a call with a professional

I am going to speak with my [accountant / lawyer / city licensing office] about [topic]. I am a [type of business] in [city], Ontario. Explain the basics of this topic in plain language, with source links. Then give me [number] specific questions I should ask, and list the documents or numbers I should have ready before the call.

Before you move on

Remember: AI can find and explain an answer in minutes, but it is only safe to rely on after you have opened the source, checked who published it and when, and confirmed it with the original authority.

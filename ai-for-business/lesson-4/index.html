Marketing Content and Ads That Stay Within the Rules

By the end of this lesson: Turn one real job or product into a week of posts, an email and ad variations, check every claim against your facts, and make sure your email and text marketing meets Canada's anti-spam law.

Lesson 3 left you with a facts sheet and checked answers. This lesson turns one real job into a week of marketing: two posts, a short email and a few ad lines. The AI multiplies what you give it. You supply the facts and do the checking.

It also covers two sets of rules that matter more once content is quick to produce: truthful advertising, and Canada's anti-spam law for email and text. You finish with content ready to post, a consent log and one number that shows whether an ad can pay for itself.

Start with one true thing, then make five pieces

Skip the request for "marketing ideas". Start from something true and yours: a job finished this month, a product that just arrived, a question a customer asked this week, the season. Give the AI tool your Lesson 3 facts sheet and plain notes about the job, and pair the result with your own photo of the work. Local detail and proof are the parts an AI tool cannot supply. Where they are missing, it fills the gap with generic wording or makes something up.

From that one job, ask for five pieces:

  • a post for your business listing
  • a social caption
  • a short email
  • a new FAQ entry
  • an ad angle

Two posts a week is about eight a month. Batch them in one sitting and that is one block of time, not eight separate scrambles.

Describe the job, not the customer. Names, addresses and contact details stay out of your notes and out of the AI tool.

Ad lines, and the number that decides whether an ad can work

Ad platforms mix and test short pieces of text, so ask for several, written to the platform's limits. Google's help page for responsive search ads lists up to 15 headlines of 30 characters and up to 4 descriptions of 90 characters (checked 6 October 2026). Ask for three angles (price, speed and trust) with a character count beside every line, and recount any line near the limit yourself, because AI tools can miscount. Then change one thing at a time, so you can tell what made the difference.

Before you spend, work out two numbers. The figures are examples only. Replace each with your own.

  • Cost per lead = ad spend ÷ leads. Example: $300 ÷ 6 enquiries = $50.
  • Break-even cost per lead = gross profit per job × close rate. Example: $400 × 1 job won from every 4 enquiries = $100. Gross profit is what the job brings in minus what it directly costs you to deliver, and close rate is the share of enquiries that turn into jobs.

In the example a lead costs $50 and the business can afford up to $100, so the ad has room. If your cost per lead sits above your break-even, better wording will not rescue the ad. Change the offer, the targeting or the landing page, or stop the ad.

Four questions before anything is published

Put every post, email and ad through four questions:

  1. Is it true? Check each price, date and claim against the facts sheet.
  2. Can I prove it to a customer standing in front of me?
  3. Am I allowed to say it in my trade?
  4. Does it reveal anything about a customer?

The first two are law. The Competition Act prohibits promoting a product or service with claims that are false or misleading in a material respect, and the Competition Bureau weighs the general impression as well as the literal words. Its guidance names specific traps: performance claims with no adequate and proper test done before the claim is made, a made-up "regular price" that dresses up a sale, a headline price that leaves out mandatory fees (government charges such as sales tax aside), and untrue low-stock or deadline claims. For a first violation, civil penalties are the ones Lesson 3 set out: up to $750,000 for an individual and $10 million for a corporation, or three times the benefit gained if that is larger. The Canadian Code of Advertising Standards also says every claim must be supported by competent and reliable evidence.

So strike "best" and "number one" unless you hold the proof. Publish a testimonial only with the customer's written approval and permission, and exactly as they gave it. Never show an AI-made image as your finished work or as a real customer. Platforms add their own rules: Meta says it puts an "AI info" label on ad images created or significantly edited with its generative AI features, or created or edited with third-party AI tools.

For question 3, regulated professions have stricter rules: the College of Physicians and Surgeons of Ontario's advertising policy, for one, bars testimonials and comparisons with other practitioners. If you answer to a regulator, read its advertising rule first. For question 4, the consent principle in federal privacy law (PIPEDA) expects meaningful consent to use personal information, and consent again for a new purpose. Alberta, British Columbia and Quebec have their own private-sector privacy laws, so check which one covers you. A photo taken for the job file is not yet cleared for an ad, so ask before showing a recognizable face, name, house number or licence plate.

This is general information, not legal advice.

Email and text: three anti-spam checks

Canada's anti-spam legislation (CASL) covers commercial messages sent to an electronic address, which the Act defines as an email, instant messaging, telephone or similar account. For you that means marketing emails, texts and direct messages. Each one must pass three checks. Public posts must still meet the truth rules above.

  1. Consent, with proof. Express consent means the person agreed, verbally or in writing, to receive your messages. It lasts until they withdraw it. Implied consent is time-limited: generally up to two years after a purchase and six months after an enquiry. Whoever claims consent has the onus of proving it, so keep a simple log: who, when, how.
  2. Identification. Each message names your business and gives a current mailing address plus a phone number, email address or web address, valid for at least 60 days after sending.
  3. Unsubscribe. Each message offers a clear, no-cost way to opt out, acted on within 10 business days at the latest. If your texts say "reply STOP", honour every STOP.

AI can write the message. It cannot create consent. Under the Act, a message asking for consent is itself a commercial message, so emailing strangers to ask permission does not get you around the rule. The Privacy Commissioner's e-marketing guidance says PIPEDA prohibits harvesting addresses with computer programs, with very limited exceptions, and that you stay responsible for the consent behind any list a supplier hands you. Treat bought and scraped lists as off limits. The maximum penalty per violation is $1 million for an individual and $10 million for a business.

This is general information, not legal advice. The rules have exceptions, so check the official pages linked here or ask a lawyer before you rely on one.

Honest limits

AI makes content cheap for everyone, your competitors included, so volume alone will not win attention. AI copy also drifts toward the same generic phrases and can get details wrong. Three habits keep it useful:

  • Check every price, date and claim against the facts sheet.
  • Read each piece aloud once. If it no longer sounds like you, rewrite it.
  • Judge marketing by enquiries and bookings, not likes.

Try it: One job, one week of marketing (about 15 minutes on a phone)

  1. Pick one real finished job, product or customer question from the last month.
  2. Give the assistant approved in your Lesson 2 policy your facts sheet and a few plain notes about the job, with no customer names, addresses or contact details. Ask for a business-listing post, a social caption, a 90-word email, and three ad headlines with one description. The first prompt below does this.
  3. Run each piece through the four-question check. Strike out any claim you could not prove to a customer standing in front of you.
  4. Run the three anti-spam checks on the email. Write down exactly which contacts are allowed to receive it and why, and start a consent log with three headings: who, when, how. Keep the log in your own records, not in the AI tool.
  5. Work out your break-even cost per lead from your own gross profit per job and close rate.
  6. Schedule or post the two posts, with your own photo of the work. Check first that the photo shows no face, name, house number or licence plate you have not been given permission to show.
  7. Finish this week: go through your whole contact list. Remove anyone whose consent you cannot account for, and invite contacts whose implied consent is still running to opt in before it runs out. Fix your message footer so it shows your business name, a mailing address, one other contact method and a working unsubscribe. Save the week's content, the consent log and your break-even number as page 4 of your AI playbook.

Prompts you can copy

One job, five pieces

Use only the facts sheet and job notes below. If a detail is missing, leave a [BLANK] for me to fill in. Do not invent prices, dates, results, reviews, awards or customer details, and do not use words such as best, number one or guaranteed. Write: (1) a business-listing post under [80] words, (2) a social caption under [50] words, (3) an email of about 90 words with a subject line, (4) one FAQ question and answer, and (5) three ad headlines of 30 characters or fewer, one each for price, speed and trust, plus one description of 90 characters or fewer. Tone: [how you talk to customers]. Area served: [city or neighbourhoods]. FACTS SHEET: [paste your facts sheet]. JOB NOTES, with no customer names or addresses: [what the job was, what you did, what makes it worth showing].

Ad lines to the character limit

Write [8] search ad headlines of 30 characters or fewer and [3] descriptions of 90 characters or fewer for [service or product] in [area served]. Cover three angles (price, speed and trust) and label the angle of each line. After each line, show its character count including spaces. Use only these facts: [paste the relevant lines of your facts sheet]. No superlatives, no guarantees, and no made-up deadlines, discounts or stock levels.

Claim check before publishing

Act as a cautious reviewer. Do not rewrite anything yet. List every factual claim in the copy below in a table with three columns: the claim, where it appears in my facts sheet (or 'not found'), and what proof a customer could ask to see. Then flag any superlative, comparison with competitors, price or discount claim, urgency wording, health or safety claim, testimonial, and anything that could identify a customer. This is a first check only; I will make the final decision. COPY: [paste the post, email or ad]. FACTS SHEET: [paste your facts sheet].

Message footer and sign-up wording

Check the marketing [email or text] below against three points and tell me what is missing. Do not judge whether I have consent; I will check that against my own records. (1) Does it clearly name my business, [business name]? (2) Does it show a mailing address and at least one of a phone number, email address or web address? (3) Does it give a clear, free way to unsubscribe? Then draft a short sign-up line for my [counter card or website form] that says what people will receive ([type of messages]), who it comes from ([business name], [mailing address], [phone, email or web address]) and that they can unsubscribe at any time. MESSAGE, with no customer details: [paste your draft].

Before you move on

Remember: AI can turn one true job into a week of marketing, but the facts, the proof and the consent have to come from you.
